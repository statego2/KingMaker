# KINGMAKER — WORLD BIBLE v1
## Canonical Setting for *KINGMAKER: The Game of Judgment*

**Status:** Canonical World Foundation v1  
**Purpose:** Provide one coherent world capable of generating the strategic mechanisms of the 1,000-question curriculum naturally through recurring actors, institutions, factions, crises and delayed consequences.

---

# 0. Canon Statement

The main campaign is set in the **Republic of Lydria**, a fictional near-future parliamentary republic at a strategic commercial and geopolitical crossroads.

The world is deliberately **realistic, modern and non-magical**.

There are:
- no superpowers in the comic-book sense,
- no hidden omnipotent conspiracy,
- no morality meter,
- no faction that is always right,
- no NPC who exists only to teach one principle,
- no reset between chapters.

The strategic difficulty comes from:

> incomplete information + conflicting incentives + institutional constraints + limited resources + adaptive human beings + time.

The world is designed so that:
- an ally today can become a rival on one issue without becoming an enemy,
- a bad person can occasionally offer a good policy,
- a trustworthy person can still become dangerous through dependency,
- a rival can become indispensable during a larger crisis,
- a good decision can fail,
- a lucky bad decision can succeed,
- a short-term victory can damage the player's long-term position,
- and the player's own successful habits can eventually become predictable and exploitable.

The campaign is not about finding "the correct law."

It is about learning to answer:

> **What is really happening?  
> What matters most?  
> What can I do now?  
> What will everyone else do next?  
> What kind of system will this create if I keep winning this way?**

# 1. High Concept

## The Republic of Lydria

Lydria is a country of roughly twelve million people positioned between:
- a wealthy continental economic bloc,
- an energy-rich authoritarian regional power,
- contested maritime trade routes,
- and one of the fastest-growing data and logistics corridors in its hemisphere.

For decades Lydria survived by being useful to everyone:
- a port for trade,
- a bridge for capital,
- a transit state for energy,
- a shipping and logistics center,
- a manufacturing base,
- and a politically reliable but occasionally difficult member of the **Continental Compact**.

That model is now breaking.

The old political class is losing legitimacy.
The economy needs a new growth model.
Automation is reshaping employment.
Energy dependence has become a national-security issue.
Media ownership is concentrated.
A massive infrastructure project could transform the country — or allow one network to capture its future chokepoints.
A larger eastern power has learned that it does not need to conquer Lydria to influence it; it merely needs Lydria to remain divided, dependent and uncertain.

At the opening of the game, a procurement scandal collapses the government.

A snap election produces **no governing majority**.

The state has a few months to:
- form a government,
- keep an expiring infrastructure-financing package alive,
- negotiate a new energy arrangement,
- prevent political paralysis from becoming economic panic,
- and decide who will control the institutions created for the next decade.

The player begins with almost no formal power.

By the end, the player may become the person through whom:
- governments are formed,
- CEOs seek access,
- factions negotiate,
- journalists verify rumours,
- foreign envoys test commitments,
- and successors are chosen.

That is the road from **Outsider → Operator → Kingmaker → Counterplayer → System Builder → Legacy**.

# 2. Why This World Fits the Curriculum

The setting must allow all 10 strategic domains to emerge naturally without jumping between unrelated fictional universes.

Lydria does this because national life is one connected network.

A port tender affects:
- corporate capital,
- unions,
- regional employment,
- foreign relations,
- media ownership,
- parliamentary votes,
- energy security,
- bank exposure,
- public trust,
- and the personal ambitions of individual actors.

A single decision can therefore produce content across the entire curriculum.

| Strategic Domain | Natural World Expression |
|---|---|
| Perception & Epistemics | leaks, intelligence, rumours, polling, audits, financial data, conflicting witnesses |
| Self-Command | humiliation, fear, euphoria, status threats, fatigue, revenge, political panic |
| Judgment & Decision | appointments, budgets, tenders, reforms, acquisitions, coalition choices |
| Timing & Execution | election windows, financing expiry, energy contracts, market runs, deadlines |
| People & Incentives | patrons, friends, rivals, employees, donors, unions, ministers, executives |
| Communication & Influence | speeches, negotiations, briefings, interviews, coalition bargaining |
| Power & Networks | parliament, boards, gatekeepers, media ownership, patronage, access, institutional vetoes |
| Reputation & Positioning | public trust, elite credibility, market perception, personal brand, factional identity |
| Conflict & Adversarial Strategy | political rivals, competitive firms, foreign pressure, information warfare, coalition splits |
| Development & Grand Strategy | institutional reform, capability building, succession, long-term national positioning |

The world therefore acts as a **strategic ecology**, not a collection of backdrops.

# 3. Genre, Tone and Technology

## Genre

**Political-economic strategic drama / institutional thriller / management RPG.**

The intended feeling is closer to:
- a serious political drama,
- a boardroom crisis,
- an intelligence briefing,
- a negotiation,
- a coalition-management simulation,

than to an action game.

Violence can exist at the edge of the world — military incidents, terrorism scares, riots, sabotage — but it is not the default interaction system.

The player's core weapon is **judgment**.

## Time

The game begins in **2032**.

This is near enough to the present to feel familiar and far enough to support:
- widespread AI copilots,
- synthetic media,
- autonomous logistics,
- real-time financial monitoring,
- more sophisticated disinformation,
- widespread remote/hybrid organizations,
- and mature data-driven campaigning.

No technology solves human incentives.

AI increases:
- information volume,
- speed,
- ability to synthesize,
- ability to manipulate,
- and the cost of knowing what is authentic.

It does not eliminate:
- trust,
- status,
- political legitimacy,
- hidden incentives,
- coalition bargaining,
- or the need for human responsibility.

# 4. Geography of Lydria

Lydria is a medium-sized coastal republic with six strategically distinct regions.

## 4.1 Velis Metropolitan Region

**Velis** is the capital and largest city.

It contains:
- Parliament,
- the Presidency,
- ministries,
- the Central Bank,
- the Constitutional Court,
- the largest national media studios,
- headquarters of the major banks,
- and most lobbying, consulting and professional networks.

Velis is where formal authority lives.

It is also where people most easily confuse visibility with power.

### Political character
Urban, highly educated, institutionally dense, status-sensitive.

### Strategic resources
- access,
- information,
- reputation,
- professional networks,
- media,
- agenda control.

---

## 4.2 The Aster Coast

Home to **Port Aster**, the country's deep-water commercial hub.

The Aster Coast carries:
- container traffic,
- fuel imports,
- naval logistics,
- subsea data cables,
- ship repair,
- and much of Lydria's export infrastructure.

It is the physical center of the proposed **Aster Gate Corridor**.

### Political character
Commercial, pragmatic, unionized, locally proud.

### Strategic resources
- logistics,
- shipping,
- energy import capacity,
- port labor,
- customs,
- foreign investment.

---

## 4.3 The Daran Industrial Belt

Lydria's old manufacturing heartland.

Major sectors:
- machinery,
- defense components,
- heavy engineering,
- automotive systems,
- industrial robotics.

Automation is making firms more competitive while threatening established employment.

### Political character
Economically anxious, union-heavy, skeptical of metropolitan elites.

### Strategic resources
- industrial capacity,
- organized labor,
- technical workforce,
- defense supply chains.

---

## 4.4 The Riva Plain

Agricultural and food-production center.

It also contains important:
- water infrastructure,
- solar fields,
- regional transport hubs,
- and smaller family businesses.

### Political character
More conservative, less institutionally connected, sensitive to energy and food costs.

### Strategic resources
- food,
- land,
- water,
- rural votes,
- distributed local networks.

---

## 4.5 The Sera Islands

A strategic island chain near important maritime routes.

The islands depend heavily on:
- tourism,
- shipping,
- fisheries,
- military bases,
- and energy links to the mainland.

Parts of the surrounding maritime zone are disputed with the neighboring **Republic of Serrat**.

### Political character
Patriotic, locally independent, economically seasonal.

### Strategic resources
- maritime claims,
- naval access,
- tourism income,
- symbolic national importance.

---

## 4.6 The Eastern Marches

Border region nearest the **Ordan Federation**.

It includes:
- energy infrastructure,
- cross-border trade,
- minority communities,
- military facilities,
- and regions with long commercial ties to Ordan.

### Political character
Mixed and often misunderstood by Velis.

### Strategic resources
- pipelines,
- border trade,
- intelligence access,
- security infrastructure,
- cross-border social networks.

# 5. The Last Twenty Years

The world should feel as if it existed before the player arrived.

## 2012 — The Debt Shock

A global downturn exposes excessive public and private borrowing.

Lydria avoids sovereign default but accepts a painful stabilization program.

Consequences that still matter in 2032:
- distrust of financial elites,
- resentment toward Continental Compact institutions,
- generational wealth divide,
- weakened public services,
- a political culture obsessed with "credibility" in financial markets.

---

## 2016 — The Port Reform

Port Aster is partially privatized.

The reform modernizes logistics and increases trade dramatically.

It also creates the first major concentration of infrastructure power around the **Aureon Consortium** and foreign capital partners.

Public lesson:
> privatization created real value and real dependency at the same time.

---

## 2022 — The Energy Winter

The Ordan Federation reduces energy exports during a regional dispute.

Lydria experiences:
- price spikes,
- industrial shutdowns,
- public anger,
- and emergency subsidies.

The event creates a national consensus that energy dependence is dangerous.

It does **not** create agreement about what should replace it.

---

## 2025 — The Integrity Reforms

A coalition government establishes:
- stronger procurement disclosure,
- an independent Integrity Commission,
- public beneficial-ownership databases,
- and new conflict-of-interest rules.

The reforms reduce crude corruption.

They also push sophisticated influence into:
- consultancies,
- informal networks,
- foundations,
- media,
- regulatory appointments,
- and lawful-but-opaque dependency structures.

Corruption becomes less obvious, not extinct.

---

## 2028 — The Automation Wave

AI and robotics increase productivity rapidly.

Benefits:
- tech investment,
- manufacturing recovery,
- new export services.

Costs:
- displacement of mid-skill office and industrial workers,
- political pressure for job protection,
- increased bargaining power for scarce technical talent.

This becomes one of the world's central tensions:

> **Growth vs social legitimacy.**

---

## 2030 — The Aster Gate Proposal

Government announces the **Aster Gate Corridor**, the largest strategic project in modern Lydrian history.

It combines:
- Port Aster expansion,
- automated freight rail,
- energy import and storage,
- a hydrogen-ready terminal,
- a subsea data-cable landing,
- a sovereign-compute/data campus,
- and upgraded defense logistics.

Estimated construction horizon: 10–12 years.

The project is not merely infrastructure.

Whoever shapes Aster Gate will influence:
- national growth,
- energy dependence,
- data sovereignty,
- labor,
- regional development,
- defense logistics,
- and the physical chokepoints of the economy.

---

## 2031 — The Harbor Contracts Scandal

Leaked procurement documents appear to show:
- consultancy overpayments,
- unusually tailored tender requirements,
- meetings omitted from official logs,
- and indirect connections to political donors.

The scandal destroys the governing coalition.

The prime minister resigns.

A snap election is called.

Important canonical fact:

> The scandal is **not** one simple conspiracy.

Some corruption is real.
Some allegations are exaggerated.
Some documents were selectively leaked.
Several factions exploit the scandal for different reasons.

This ambiguity becomes the first lesson of the game:
**true facts can be used to produce a false total picture.**

# 6. Constitutional Order

Lydria is a parliamentary republic.

## 6.1 The Assembly

**240 seats.**  
A government requires **121** votes of confidence.

At game start:

| Bloc | Seats |
|---|---:|
| Renewal Alliance | 68 |
| Civic Labour | 52 |
| Stability Union | 46 |
| Sovereign Front | 38 |
| Free Cities List | 22 |
| Regional / Independents | 14 |
| **Total** | **240** |

No two major blocs can govern comfortably without either:
- a third actor,
- independents,
- defections,
- or a formal coalition.

This deliberately makes coalition strategy central to the world.

---

## 6.2 The Presidency

The President is head of state and constitutional guardian.

Formal powers are limited but strategically significant:
- nominates a prime-ministerial candidate after party consultations,
- can return legislation for reconsideration,
- appoints some independent-institution leaders from shortlists,
- convenes the National Council in crises,
- possesses tightly bounded emergency authority,
- and is one of the few actors with legitimate access across all major factions.

The President does not govern day to day.

But in a fragmented parliament, **access, legitimacy and timing** make the Presidency far more important than its formal powers suggest.

---

## 6.3 The Prime Minister and Cabinet

The Prime Minister controls:
- executive agenda,
- cabinet appointments,
- budget priorities,
- inter-ministerial coordination.

Weakness:
a coalition PM may possess broad formal authority but little freedom if:
- coalition partners control key ministries,
- parliamentary committees can block,
- coalition agreement restricts appointments,
- and informal patrons influence implementation.

---

## 6.4 Constitutional Court

Independent but not politically invisible.

It can:
- invalidate laws,
- review emergency actions,
- settle institutional disputes.

The Court cannot simply be "captured" by one choice.

Its legitimacy can, however, be gradually damaged by:
- appointment wars,
- selective compliance,
- public delegitimization,
- procedural manipulation.

---

## 6.5 Central Bank of Lydria

Operationally independent.

It monitors:
- bank liquidity,
- systemic credit risk,
- inflation,
- financial stability.

It does not control fiscal policy.

The Central Bank often possesses information the elected government does not yet want public.

This creates recurring tensions around:
- secrecy vs public reassurance,
- technocratic independence vs democratic accountability,
- and market credibility vs political flexibility.

---

## 6.6 Integrity Commission

Created after the 2025 reforms.

It can investigate:
- conflicts of interest,
- procurement irregularities,
- undisclosed beneficial ownership,
- certain public-official financial relationships.

It has strong investigative legitimacy but limited enforcement power.

It therefore depends on:
- prosecutors,
- courts,
- parliament,
- public credibility.

This makes it powerful but vulnerable to politicization.

---

## 6.7 National Intelligence Directorate

A civilian intelligence service responsible for:
- foreign influence,
- counterintelligence,
- strategic security,
- major cyber-threat attribution.

It is intentionally not omniscient.

Reports have:
- source reliability,
- confidence,
- competing interpretations,
- and institutional incentives.

An intelligence report is an input, not a magic truth card.

---

# 7. The National Strategy Office

This institution exists specifically to anchor the player's campaign.

## Before the game

During the election transition, the Presidency creates a temporary **Strategic Coordination Secretariat (SCS)** to synthesize:
- coalition risks,
- national-security briefings,
- Aster Gate deadlines,
- energy negotiations,
- and continuity planning.

The player enters the story as a relatively unknown analyst inside this small unit.

The player initially possesses:
- access to information,
- almost no decision authority,
- almost no public reputation,
- and no personal factional machine.

That is deliberate.

The player's early power comes from:
- seeing things others miss,
- earning trust,
- becoming useful,
- and learning where formal authority differs from actual influence.

## After government formation

One possible coalition compromise turns the temporary unit into the **National Strategy Office (NSO)**.

The office is created because no coalition partner trusts another to control long-range coordination alone.

Its remit can include:
- strategic-risk analysis,
- cross-ministry project coordination,
- crisis dashboards,
- scenario planning,
- implementation monitoring.

The NSO has little direct command authority.

Its potential power comes from:
- information aggregation,
- access,
- agenda preparation,
- cross-faction relationships,
- and the ability to connect otherwise separate institutions.

That makes it an ideal institutional home for a future kingmaker.

It is also dangerous.

An NSO can become:
- a useful coordination node,
- an informal shadow government,
- a captured intelligence filter,
- or an institution that becomes too dependent on the player personally.

The campaign must eventually force the player to decide which.

# 8. Political Factions

No faction is a morality archetype.

Each has:
- legitimate goals,
- real constituencies,
- blind spots,
- internal divisions,
- and actors with mixed motives.

---

## 8.1 Renewal Alliance — 68 seats

### Public identity
Reformist, technocratic, pro-growth, anti-corruption.

### Core constituencies
- urban professionals,
- entrepreneurs,
- younger managers,
- reform-minded civil servants,
- parts of the diaspora.

### Wants
- institutional modernization,
- digital government,
- Aster Gate,
- deeper Continental Compact integration,
- investment and productivity.

### Fears
- return of patronage politics,
- fiscal instability,
- international loss of credibility.

### Internal contradiction
One wing believes:
> good institutions will naturally create political support.

Another believes:
> institutions matter only if you first acquire enough power to protect them.

This split is central to the player's world.

---

## 8.2 Civic Labour — 52 seats

### Public identity
Social-democratic, pro-labor, pro-public services.

### Core constituencies
- organized labor,
- public employees,
- industrial regions,
- lower-income urban voters.

### Wants
- wage security,
- stronger public services,
- worker participation in automation policy,
- regional investment.

### Fears
- technology-driven inequality,
- privatization without safeguards,
- a government run by technocrats and corporate networks.

### Internal contradiction
Its reform wing understands productivity is necessary.

Its machine wing benefits from some of the same old institutional dependencies it publicly criticizes.

---

## 8.3 Stability Union — 46 seats

### Public identity
Center-right, experienced, pro-business, order and continuity.

### Core constituencies
- older voters,
- provincial business networks,
- property owners,
- parts of the professional establishment.

### Wants
- predictable government,
- investment,
- strong defense,
- gradual reform.

### Fears
- radical institutional experiments,
- fiscal instability,
- coalition chaos.

### Internal contradiction
It contains some of the country's most capable administrators.

It is also the party most damaged by the Harbor Contracts scandal and by longstanding patronage networks.

---

## 8.4 Sovereign Front — 38 seats

### Public identity
National sovereignty, security, protection of domestic industry and borders.

### Core constituencies
- security-minded voters,
- parts of the military/veteran community,
- industrial protectionists,
- culturally conservative regions.

### Wants
- energy autonomy,
- harder line against Ordan and Serrat,
- domestic ownership of strategic assets,
- stronger executive capacity.

### Fears
- foreign control,
- national humiliation,
- elite detachment,
- demographic decline.

### Internal contradiction
Some members are serious national-security realists.

Others convert every compromise into a loyalty test and every disagreement into betrayal.

---

## 8.5 Free Cities List — 22 seats

### Public identity
Urban liberal, green, digital-rights, transparency-first.

### Core constituencies
- younger metropolitan voters,
- creative/technology workers,
- academics,
- anti-corruption activists.

### Wants
- radical transparency,
- decentralized government,
- climate transition,
- civil liberties,
- open digital systems.

### Fears
- surveillance,
- oligarchic capture,
- corruption,
- old-party patronage.

### Internal contradiction
Its transparency instincts often improve institutions.

They can also collide with:
- negotiation secrecy,
- national security,
- personal privacy,
- and the reality that not every unfinished internal discussion should be public.

---

## 8.6 Regional and Independent Members — 14 seats

These are not one faction.

They include:
- island representatives,
- agricultural independents,
- local reformers,
- defectors from larger parties.

They are often decisive.

Their importance makes them targets for:
- genuine policy bargaining,
- local investment,
- patronage,
- media pressure,
- and coalition wedge strategies.

# 9. Non-Party Power Centers

## 9.1 Aureon Consortium

An old Lydrian conglomerate with interests in:
- construction,
- shipping,
- logistics,
- energy infrastructure,
- commercial property,
- and a minority stake in a major media network.

Aureon is neither simply corrupt nor simply productive.

It has:
- real execution capacity,
- deep institutional memory,
- capital,
- political access.

Its strategic objective is not merely profit from Aster Gate construction.

It wants influence over the **chokepoints**:
- port operations,
- freight access,
- energy storage,
- land concessions.

The more infrastructure becomes indispensable, the more bargaining power Aureon gains.

---

## 9.2 Nexa Systems

Fast-growing Lydrian technology group.

Specializes in:
- AI systems,
- public-sector software,
- automation,
- cybersecurity,
- data infrastructure.

Public image:
modern, clean, meritocratic.

Real vulnerability:
some core cloud and semiconductor dependencies remain foreign.

Nexa publicly argues for "Lydrian digital sovereignty" while depending on external infrastructure more than it admits.

This is not necessarily hypocrisy.
It is a strategic contradiction.

---

## 9.3 Meridian Bank

Largest domestically headquartered bank.

Strong public image.
Deep corporate relationships.
Systemically important.

Its hidden vulnerability:
large exposure to:
- property,
- infrastructure lending,
- and a small number of large corporate groups.

At game start Meridian Bank is **not insolvent**.

But it is more fragile than the public believes.

A panic could make a manageable balance-sheet problem become a real liquidity crisis.

---

## 9.4 Federation of Lydrian Labour

Umbrella organization for major unions.

Public position:
protect jobs from automation and privatization.

Private reality is more nuanced.

Many leaders understand automation cannot simply be banned.

Their true bargaining goals often include:
- transition guarantees,
- retraining,
- worker board representation,
- regional investment,
- stronger severance rules,
- and a seat in decisions before they are announced.

This allows negotiation rather than treating "labor" as a fixed anti-growth actor.

---

## 9.5 Vela Media Network

Largest private broadcast and digital news group.

Not a single ideological machine.

Different editors and presenters have different views.

However:
- ownership matters,
- access matters,
- ratings matter,
- advertiser relationships matter,
- elite social networks matter.

Vela can shape:
- issue salience,
- who receives legitimacy through airtime,
- what becomes a "crisis",
- and whose framing becomes the default question.

It cannot simply make the public believe anything.

---

## 9.6 Civic Wire

Small but influential investigative media platform.

Known for:
- procurement investigations,
- data journalism,
- source protection,
- aggressive transparency.

Strength:
credibility among professionals and younger voters.

Weakness:
its incentives favor uncovering hidden wrongdoing.

That means it can sometimes over-weight:
- scandal,
- opacity,
- and individual malice,

when the real cause is incompetence, ambiguity or institutional dysfunction.

---

# 10. External Actors

## 10.1 The Ordan Federation

Large eastern power.

Characteristics:
- more authoritarian political system,
- major energy exporter,
- significant military capability,
- state-linked corporate networks,
- sophisticated influence apparatus.

### Strategic objective toward Lydria

Ordan does not need direct control.

It benefits if Lydria is:
- divided,
- energy-dependent,
- skeptical of the Continental Compact,
- hesitant in regional alliances,
- and internally uncertain about what is true.

Its influence tools can include:
- discounted energy,
- investment,
- commercial relationships,
- media narratives,
- diplomatic pressure,
- cultivation of elites,
- cyber/intelligence activity.

Important rule:

> Not every Lydrian actor who favors cooperation with Ordan is an Ordan agent.

Many simply have:
- commercial interests,
- regional ties,
- ideological views,
- or different assessments of national interest.

---

## 10.2 Republic of Serrat

A neighboring parliamentary republic and commercial competitor.

Relations with Lydria are mixed.

Cooperation:
- trade,
- tourism,
- regional security,
- disaster response.

Conflict:
- maritime zones,
- port competition,
- fisheries,
- energy exploration.

Neither country wants war.

Domestic political incentives can nevertheless turn small disputes into status contests.

Serrat is designed to produce:
- deterrence problems,
- signaling,
- tactical cooperation,
- and situations where today's rival is tomorrow's necessary ally.

---

## 10.3 The Continental Compact

A multinational democratic economic and security bloc.

Lydria is a member but retains:
- its own currency,
- substantial fiscal sovereignty,
- and independent foreign-policy discretion.

The Compact provides:
- market access,
- infrastructure funds,
- regulatory frameworks,
- security coordination.

It also imposes:
- fiscal standards,
- procurement rules,
- legal obligations,
- anti-corruption conditions.

Compact institutions are not benevolent parents.

They have their own:
- political constraints,
- bureaucratic incentives,
- member-state interests.

---

# 11. The Aster Gate Corridor

Aster Gate is the world's central strategic asset.

It should recur throughout the campaign in different forms rather than exist as one mission.

## Components

1. Deep-water port expansion
2. Automated freight terminal
3. High-speed freight rail to the Daran Belt
4. LNG / hydrogen-ready energy terminal
5. Strategic fuel storage
6. Subsea data-cable landing
7. Sovereign-compute/data campus
8. Defense logistics upgrades
9. Industrial free-zone redevelopment
10. New regional power interconnector

## Why everyone wants influence

### Renewal Alliance
Growth and modernization.

### Civic Labour
Jobs, worker protections, regional investment.

### Stability Union
Investment continuity and business confidence.

### Sovereign Front
Domestic control of strategic infrastructure.

### Free Cities List
Transparency, environmental safeguards, data rights.

### Aureon
Infrastructure control and long-term concession value.

### Nexa
Digital infrastructure and government contracts.

### Ordan
Avoid exclusion from Lydrian energy/data systems.

### Continental Compact
Keep the corridor integrated with its standards and financing ecosystem.

### Serrat
Prevent Aster Gate from permanently eclipsing its own ports.

Aster Gate is therefore not merely a "project."

It is a **network-power generator**.

# 12. Information Ecology

The game must treat information as an object with provenance.

Every important claim can have:

```text
FACT
SOURCE
SOURCE ACCESS
SOURCE INCENTIVE
PROVENANCE
INDEPENDENCE
CONFIDENCE
DISTORTION
VISIBILITY
WHO BELIEVES IT
WHO KNOWS THAT OTHERS BELIEVE IT
```

## Information channels

- intelligence briefs
- cabinet notes
- board documents
- journalist calls
- private messages
- polls
- market data
- financial filings
- whistleblowers
- audits
- diplomatic cables
- social media
- public speeches
- informal social networks

## Canonical information rule

A document can be authentic and still be misleading.

A source can be biased and still be correct.

A trusted ally can pass false information sincerely.

A rival can release true information for manipulative reasons.

The player must learn to distinguish:
- truth,
- interpretation,
- intent,
- and effect.

# 13. Media and Public Narrative

Public opinion is not one bar.

At minimum, the game should track:
- general public trust,
- coalition-base sentiment,
- elite/business confidence,
- labor sentiment,
- youth/urban sentiment,
- regional sentiment,
- international investor perception.

A narrative can therefore:
- hurt the player with one audience,
- help with another,
- and be irrelevant to a third.

## Narrative mechanics

A public story has:
- salience,
- credibility,
- emotional intensity,
- repetition,
- elite endorsement,
- evidence strength,
- counter-narrative availability,
- decay rate.

The player should not be encouraged to answer every attack.

Some attacks grow when answered.

Others become dangerous when ignored.

# 15. Strategic Currencies

The player should think in more than money.

The world contains several currencies of power:

## Capital
Money, budget, credit, investment.

## Attention
Time of leaders, media, public, experts.

## Information
Reliable knowledge before others have it.

## Access
Ability to reach decision makers.

## Credibility
How seriously others take your claims and commitments.

## Goodwill
Accumulated willingness to help.

## Political Capital
Ability to ask allies to take costly action.

## Institutional Authority
Formal right to decide.

## Network Centrality
Ability to connect actors who otherwise cannot coordinate.

## Optionality
Ability to change course later.

## Legitimacy
Whether actors accept an outcome as rightful enough to endure.

A key design principle:

> No single currency dominates every arena.

# 16. Starting Political Equilibrium

The election has just ended.

No government exists.

The outgoing administration is in caretaker status.

## Immediate public facts

- Parliament is hung.
- Harbor Contracts scandal remains unresolved.
- Aster Gate financing terms expire in roughly three months.
- Ordan energy contract expires shortly afterward.
- Business confidence is slipping.
- Coalition talks have begun.
- Public distrust is high.

## Less-visible facts

- Meridian Bank is experiencing small but unusual corporate withdrawals.
- Aureon is lobbying multiple factions simultaneously.
- Nexa is negotiating privately with a foreign cloud provider.
- Labour leadership is more open to automation compromise than public rhetoric suggests.
- Ordan-linked commercial actors are funding several mutually contradictory narratives.
- Serrat is considering a new port-subsidy package.
- multiple factions want control of the future National Strategy Office before it even formally exists.

# 17. Day Zero: The Player's Position

The player is intentionally not born powerful.

## Formal role

**Junior strategic analyst** in the temporary Strategic Coordination Secretariat.

## Initial advantages

- analytical access across several institutional silos,
- no established public ideological identity,
- permission to read material most ordinary party staff do not see,
- proximity to actors involved in government formation.

## Initial weaknesses

- no vote,
- no budget,
- no staff,
- no media profile,
- no personal political machine,
- no wealthy patron,
- no guaranteed future employment.

## Initial strategic asset

The player can become useful to multiple actors without initially threatening them.

That window will not last forever.

As reputation grows:
- some actors will seek the player,
- some will attempt to capture the player,
- some will fear the player,
- and some will deliberately feed the player information.

This is how early learning evolves into late-game adversarial play.

# 18. The Six Campaign Eras

This is not yet the detailed Story Architecture.

It is the **world-level progression** the later story must respect.

---

## ACT I — THE OUTSIDER

### World condition
Government formation.

### Player condition
Information access without authority.

### Natural dilemmas
- who is credible,
- who actually controls votes,
- which leaks are real,
- how factions differ internally,
- when to speak,
- whom to trust.

### Mastery emphasis
Recognition + Diagnosis.

### Central feeling
> "I thought the organization chart showed who had power. It doesn't."

---

## ACT II — THE OPERATOR

### World condition
A fragile coalition forms.

### Player condition
The player receives responsibility for coordination or a significant strategic portfolio.

### Natural dilemmas
- appointments,
- budgets,
- Aster Gate,
- labor negotiations,
- implementation,
- crisis communication.

### Mastery emphasis
Application.

### Central feeling
> "Seeing the right answer is easier than making a system do it."

---

## ACT III — THE KINGMAKER

### World condition
Coalition fractures become more important.

### Player condition
The player's network now crosses parties, business, media and institutions.

### Natural dilemmas
- loyalty vs independence,
- leverage,
- side deals,
- succession,
- media positioning,
- coalition survival.

### Mastery emphasis
Trade-off.

### Central feeling
> "Every gain now costs something I also value."

---

## ACT IV — THE COUNTERPLAYER

### World condition
Opponents stop treating the player as an analyst and begin treating them as a strategic actor.

### Player condition
Habits are visible.

### Natural dilemmas
- bait,
- planted information,
- coalition wedges,
- manufactured deadlines,
- reputation traps,
- adaptive rivals.

### Mastery emphasis
Adversarial.

### Central feeling
> "The other side has learned how I think."

---

## ACT V — THE SYSTEM

### World condition
Multiple systems enter crisis or transition at once.

Potential combination:
- Meridian Bank liquidity pressure,
- Aster Gate decision,
- energy negotiation,
- coalition collapse,
- external maritime crisis,
- labor unrest.

### Player condition
The player has enough influence that every intervention creates secondary effects.

### Mastery emphasis
Integrated / Multi-System.

### Central feeling
> "There is no isolated decision anymore."

---

## ACT VI — LEGACY

### World condition
The immediate crises have either been survived or transformed.

### Player condition
The player may be personally indispensable.

### Final strategic question

> Do you use peak influence to become harder to remove,
> or to build a system that needs you less?

### Mastery emphasis
Adaptive Mastery / Legacy.

### Central feeling
> "What remains after I am gone?"

# 19. Persistent World Tracks

The following are world-level variables, not visible perfect numbers to the player.

## Government Stability
Ability of executive coalition to survive votes and govern.

## Public Trust
General belief that institutions act competently and legitimately.

## Institutional Integrity
Strength of neutral rules, truth flow and non-personalized governance.

## Fiscal Headroom
Ability to absorb shocks or invest without crisis.

## Financial Stability
Health of banks, credit and market confidence.

## Energy Security
Diversification, reserves, infrastructure resilience.

## Social Cohesion
Degree to which conflicts remain governable rather than identity-based.

## External Pressure
Combined diplomatic, economic, intelligence and security pressure.

## Information Quality
How accurately important information moves through institutions.

## Strategic Autonomy
How many credible options Lydria has in energy, finance, technology and alliances.

## Growth Capacity
Ability to generate future productivity, investment and employment.

These tracks should interact.

Example:
- hiding bad news may temporarily raise Government Stability,
- while lowering Information Quality and Institutional Integrity,
- increasing probability of larger future crisis.

# 22. Memory Model

The world must remember **events**, not just totals.

A memory should contain:

```text
event_id
actor
player_action
actor_interpretation
public_interpretation
intent_believed
cost_to_actor
benefit_to_actor
was_promise_kept
was_information_truthful
was_actor_humiliated
was_actor_protected
was_credit_shared
time
decay_rate
salience
```

This allows two crucial effects.

## Attribution

NPCs do not only remember what happened.

They remember **why they think you did it**.

A beneficial action attributed to manipulation may increase dependency but not trust.

## Delayed recall

A choice in Act I can become relevant in Act IV.

Example:
you protect an ally from public humiliation early.

Years later they may:
- remember loyalty,
- owe you political capital,
- or believe you are conflict-averse and exploit that pattern.

The same memory can produce both benefit and vulnerability.

# 24. Hidden Canonical Truths

These are **developer truths**, not immediately known by the player.

They exist to prevent the world from becoming arbitrary.

## Truth 1 — The Harbor Contracts scandal is mixed

There was genuine improper influence.

There was not one centralized conspiracy.

Several actors selectively leaked real evidence for different strategic reasons.

---

## Truth 2 — Ordan's preferred outcome is fragmentation, not invasion

A weak, divided Lydria that cannot form durable strategy is more useful to Ordan than a directly controlled client state.

This means influence operations can support contradictory factions simultaneously.

---

## Truth 3 — Aureon's real Aster Gate prize is chokepoint control

Construction profit matters.

Long-term control of:
- logistics,
- concessions,
- storage,
- land,
- and switching costs

matters more.

---

## Truth 4 — Nexa's sovereignty story is incomplete

Nexa genuinely wants a domestic digital stack.

But it currently cannot provide one without foreign cloud/chip dependencies.

Its executives disagree on how openly to admit this.

---

## Truth 5 — Meridian Bank is vulnerable, not doomed

At opening state, a bank run is avoidable.

Poor communication or political exploitation can create the crisis that people fear.

---

## Truth 6 — Labour leadership can accept automation

The real red lines are:
- surprise,
- unilateralism,
- no retraining,
- no distribution of productivity gains,
- no voice.

This makes negotiated reform possible.

---

## Truth 7 — The Presidency's central fear is institutional collapse

The President will sometimes oppose the player's preferred outcome if the method threatens constitutional legitimacy.

This is not necessarily personal betrayal.

---

## Truth 8 — Public opinion is more fluid than party elites believe

Many voters hold combinations of beliefs that do not fit party packages.

Issue coalitions can be rebuilt.

---

## Truth 9 — Serrat does not want a major conflict

But Serrat's government can become trapped by the same domestic status incentives as Lydria.

Escalation can emerge without either side initially wanting it.

---

## Truth 10 — The greatest long-term risk is personalized competence

A brilliant strategist can stabilize a weak system so effectively that everyone becomes more dependent on the strategist.

The player can therefore "win" the entire campaign while leaving Lydria weaker.

This is the core Legacy problem.

# 25. Opening Clocks

At campaign start several clocks are running.

## Government Formation

## Aster Gate Financing
A favorable Continental Compact financing package has an expiry window.

## Energy Contract
The Ordan supply agreement is approaching renegotiation.

## Banking Confidence
Meridian Bank has mild outflows that can stabilize or accelerate.

## Labour Automation Pact
An existing labor agreement expires within the first year.

## Serrat Maritime Talks
A scheduled negotiation could reduce tension — or become a campaign issue.

These clocks create real timing conflicts.

The player cannot maximize all of them simultaneously.

# 27. Event Conversion Rules

The 1,000 workbook cases should be converted according to:

```text
STRATEGIC MECHANISM
        ↓
WHICH EXISTING ACTORS CAN NATURALLY CREATE IT?
        ↓
WHICH CURRENT WORLD PRESSURE MAKES IT MATTER NOW?
        ↓
WHAT INFORMATION DOES THE PLAYER ACTUALLY HAVE?
        ↓
WHAT DOES EACH ACTOR BELIEVE?
        ↓
PLAYER DECISION
        ↓
IMMEDIATE REACTION
        ↓
DELAYED MEMORY / POWER / REPUTATION EFFECT
        ↓
NEW WORLD STATE
```

Example:

Workbook:
> trusted source has new conflict of interest.

Campaign:
> a journalist who helped the player in Act I later becomes financially tied to Vela Network.

The lesson is not announced.

The player simply encounters a known person whose incentive structure changed.

# 28. What the Player Can Build

The player's progress should not only be personal statistics.

The player can gradually build assets.

## Intelligence Network
More sources, better provenance, wider domains.

Risk:
more sources also create noise, leaks and source-management costs.

## Coalition Network
Ability to connect actors.

Risk:
becoming indispensable creates dependency and suspicion.

## Staff
Analysts, negotiators, operators.

Risk:
poor delegation creates bottlenecks or internal factions.

## Credibility
Others trust your claims.

Risk:
credibility can become a trap if you become afraid to update publicly.

## Institutional Capacity
Processes that function without you.

This is the highest-level asset.

## Successors
People capable of carrying the system.

This is the final test of whether the player's power has matured.

# 29. Core World Tensions

The 15 Master Tensions should appear at the world level, not only in individual decisions.

### Speed ↔ Deliberation
Crisis state vs constitutional process.

### Boldness ↔ Caution
Aster Gate expansion vs financial resilience.

### Persistence ↔ Exit
Failing projects, coalitions, alliances.

### Transparency ↔ Secrecy
Public trust vs negotiation/intelligence security.

### Consistency ↔ Adaptability
Political mandate vs changing conditions.

### Visibility ↔ Scarcity
Public leadership vs overexposure.

### Confidence ↔ Doubt
Markets and voters demand clarity under uncertainty.

### Independence ↔ Commitment
Coalitions, alliances, financing.

### Concentration ↔ Redundancy
Single suppliers, star employees, strategic partnerships.

### Kindness ↔ Deterrence
Allies, rivals, repeated boundary tests.

### Authenticity ↔ Adaptation
Personal style vs heterogeneous audiences.

### Principle ↔ Pragmatism
Institutional rules vs immediate achievable outcomes.

### Victory ↔ Restraint
What happens after you win.

### Predictability ↔ Trust
Reliable commitments vs exploitable patterns.

### Power ↔ Legitimacy
The central tension of the entire campaign.

# 30. World-Level Reversal Logic

The game should repeatedly teach that the same action changes value when context changes.

Example:

**Transparency**
- early scandal: more transparency may rebuild trust,
- active negotiation: too much transparency destroys bargaining position,
- security flaw: disclosure timing must protect users,
- mature institution: procedural transparency may matter more than tactical disclosure.

The player should experience the same principle:
- succeeding once,
- becoming habitual,
- then failing under changed conditions.

That is how the campaign converts knowledge into adaptive mastery.

# 31. Failure States

There should be very few instant "game over" screens.

Instead, failure usually means:
- lost leverage,
- damaged trust,
- weaker coalition,
- depleted reserves,
- bad institutional precedent,
- public narrative shift,
- rival empowerment.

Possible hard failure conditions:
- constitutional breakdown,
- state insolvency,
- catastrophic coalition/war outcome,
- complete loss of player role through scandal or removal.

Even then, some branches may continue from a weaker position rather than simply end.

# 32. End-State Axes

The ending should not be one good/bad score.

Evaluate at least:

## Personal Power
How much direct influence does the player retain?

## Institutional Strength
Can the system function without them?

## Legitimacy
Are decisions accepted as sufficiently fair and lawful?

## Economic Resilience
Can Lydria absorb shocks?

## Strategic Autonomy
Does Lydria possess real choices?

## Social Cohesion
Can disagreement remain political rather than existential?

## Truth Flow
Can bad news reach leaders?

## Successor Quality
Are capable independent people ready to take over?

## Coalition Ecology
Did the player create durable cooperation or permanent dependency?

## Integrity / Legacy
What norms did the player's methods teach everyone else?

The most interesting ending can therefore be:

> **High personal power, low institutional strength.**

The player "wins" but the system is fragile.

Or:

> **Lower personal centrality, high institutional strength.**

The player may look less dominant but has achieved deeper strategic mastery.

# 33. Anti-Cliché Rules

The Character Bible and Story Architecture must obey these.

1. No faction is secretly evil as a whole.
2. No billionaire, intelligence chief or politician controls everything.
3. Corruption usually emerges through incentives, dependencies and networks — not moustache-twirling villains.
4. The public is not stupid.
5. Experts can be wrong.
6. Populists can identify real problems.
7. Technocrats can hide value judgments behind "neutral expertise."
8. Allies can disagree honestly.
9. Rivals can make valid arguments.
10. Institutional procedure can be both protective and obstructive.
11. Transparency can help or harm depending on what is disclosed to whom and when.
12. Secrecy can be legitimate or abusive.
13. Strong leadership can solve coordination problems and also create dependency.
14. Decentralization can protect pluralism and also produce paralysis.
15. A clean motive does not guarantee a good decision.
16. A selfish motive does not make a proposal false.
17. Every major actor must have at least one situation where working with them is rational.
18. Every major actor must have at least one situation where trusting them too much is dangerous.
19. No recurring NPC exists only to betray the player.
20. Consequences should emerge from prior states, not writer punishment.

# 34. Tone Rules for Dialogue and Scenes

NPC dialogue should sound like people protecting:
- interests,
- identity,
- status,
- values,
- relationships,
- and uncertainty.

Avoid:
- villains explaining their schemes,
- NPCs naming strategy principles,
- philosophical monologues inserted only to teach,
- "you chose the wise option" feedback.

Instead:

> "If you force the vote tonight, I can give you six people. Tomorrow? Maybe nine. But once this is on television, I can't promise you any of them."

This line naturally encodes:
- timing,
- coalition math,
- reputation,
- uncertainty,
- and commitment.

The strategy lives inside the world.

# 36. Initial World-State Philosophy

The opening world is deliberately **stable enough to save and unstable enough to matter**.

No collapse is predetermined.

If the player makes excellent decisions:
- the bank run may never occur,
- the maritime dispute may de-escalate,
- the coalition may survive,
- Aster Gate may become a national asset.

If the player makes different choices:
- the same latent vulnerabilities can become crises.

This is essential.

The game should not pretend player agency matters while forcing every major crisis to occur identically.

Some events are structural.
Their **form, timing and severity** must remain state-dependent.

9. Non-party power is structurally important.
